FlexNode

One module per joint. Each closes a high-current motor loop millimetres from the windings, reads the sensors around it, and answers to a single CAN-FD pair — so building a robot that moves and senses well stops being an electronics project and becomes a shopping list.

The idea

Most of the work in a walking robot isn't the walking. Every joint needs a motor controller; every controller needs encoder input, current sensing, thermal protection and a wire home. Add a foot sensor, a rangefinder or an IMU and that is another board, another connector, another protocol — and none of it is the thing you set out to build.

FlexNode collapses all of that into one module you buy N of: a field-oriented motor controller and a sensor front end on the same small PCB, hanging off a single CAN-FD pair. Bolt one to the back of each actuator, chain them together, and the electronics are finished. The goal is that any sensor-fused robot reduces to buy FlexNodes + actuators + sensors, wire power and CAN — no board design at all.

A node, not a motor driver

The power stage is the easy half. Around it sit a 6-axis IMU, an optional 8×8 ranging sensor, an encoder port, a current-sensed 5 V servo output and SPI/GPIO breakouts — all reported through the same protocol as the motor. A joint that also has to know its foot is loaded doesn't need a second board.

The loop closes at the joint

Each node runs its own tens-of-kHz current loop millimetres from the windings and the encoder. The host only plans. Nothing time-critical has to survive a trip across the robot.

Identical boards, one image

What a node is — its address, its encoder source, which peripherals it carries — is per-node configuration, not code. Ten actuators means ten identical boards, two wires between them and a config file each. No per-node builds, ever.

Safe when the brain isn't

Hosts crash, hang and drop into low power. Nodes don't coast into a wall when commands stop arriving — they step through defined states down to a safe hold on their own, without depending on the thing that just died.

Every motor, measurable

The node driving a motor is the best-placed thing to know its health. Bus voltage, all three phase currents, FET temperature — and a winding-resistance fit, which copper's temperature coefficient turns into a winding-temperature estimate even on motors with no thermistor at all.

Open, on proven silicon

A fork of the mjbots moteus r4.11 — its power stage, gate driver and STM32G4 core kept intact, its FOC core untouched. Open hardware under Apache-2.0, schematics and gerbers in the repo.

The design target is CatBot, a lightweight jumping quadruped: ten FlexNodes, one per actuator, chained back to a corenode and an onboard Jetson. Split the chain left/right and a single bus fault takes down one side of the robot rather than all of it.

Bring-up · September 2026

Where it stands

Electronics and thermals fully validated — September 2026.

First boot, 6 September 2026. A v1.0 board came up on a 13 V bench adapter with no motor and no encoder fitted. Both logic rails measured stable, the MCU boots from flash, and the moteus → FlexNode port runs: steady status LED, servo reporting stopped. Later the same day the onboard IMU answered over I²C and streamed samples — verified by tilting the board and watching the status LED shift hue, no CAN adapter needed.

First spin, 8 September 2026. Two days later the power stage was energized for the first time and a GIM 8108-8 turned under FlexNode's own drive: open-loop voltage FOC, no encoder in the loop, from a supply that current-limits at ~1 A so the bench — not the winding — was the thing that could give out. The stage that mattered ran eight movement loops, ~140 s energized, zero faults, the FETs plateauing at 38.7 °C. Then a deliberate over-demand asked for more than the adapter could give: the bus collapsed, and the software abort caught it and de-energized cleanly. A protection path exercised for real rather than merely written.

The sensing came out of it validated too. All three current-sense channels calibrated, and their phase currents sum to about zero — which checks the sense chain and the phase wiring together. Bus-voltage telemetry was confirmed against a meter and trimmed. The power stage and every sensing path on the board are now hardware-validated. The encoder isn't fitted yet, so nothing closed-loop has ever run, and CAN is untested for want of an adapter.

Powering it safely

Bus voltage: up to ~44 V (10S LiPo). Bus ceramics are rated 50 V and the power FETs 60 V — sized for that rail, with no margin to spare above it.

⚠ Never hot-plug a FlexNode onto a live pack. Cable inductance and the ceramic bus form an LC tank that can ring toward 2× and blow past the 60 V FETs. Always bring the bus up through the core board's pre-charge — or a current-limited supply on the bench.

⚠ Do not flash unmodified moteus. The motor phase A/C outputs are swapped in copper relative to stock and the strap pins are repurposed. FlexNode's firmware compensates for both; stock firmware would close the current loop on the wrong winding.

Status: v1.0 is in bring-up. The power stage and sensing are hardware-validated; closed-loop control and CAN are not. Everything here remains subject to change until formal validation is complete.

Firmware & the CAN layer

The firmware is a fork of moteus that keeps the FOC inner loop, the FDCAN protocol and the bootloader byte-for-byte. FlexNode's changes are small and deliberately off the control path: a board-identity hardcode, an encoder chip-select remap, and a two-line drive-side pin remap that corrects the board's phase swap without touching a line of control code. On top of that sit the status and lighting driver, the IMU and the FlexNode register block. Flash a virgin board over the FLASH header with SWD; after that, updates go over CAN with moteus_tool, exactly as a moteus does. Step-by-step in docs/firmware.md.

The CAN layer extends the moteus register protocol rather than inventing one, so moteus_tool, tview and the CAN bootloader all keep working. A node presents as one axis plus a set of typed channel slots that the host reads at boot — supporting a new kind of peripheral adds an enum value rather than a register range, and one host driver covers every node in the fleet. Each query is composed per node: the host asks only for the hardware that node actually carries. Traffic is strictly poll-response on a schedule the master owns, so no node ever transmits unbidden; if the master goes quiet, the graded host-loss states take over. Ten nodes on one ~45 cm chain works out to roughly 350 Hz at two-thirds bus occupancy.

None of the CAN work has been exercised over a real bus yet — there is no adapter on the bench, and the register block has never been read. The full spec is in docs/can-layer.md.
